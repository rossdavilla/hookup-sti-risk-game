About The Hook-Up Game

An open, transparent look at how this educational tool estimates STI risk — so educators can check the math and the sources before using it with students.

What this is

The Hook-Up Game lets a student describe a year of sexual choices — their anatomy and their partners' anatomy, what kind of partners (relationship, friend, acquaintance, stranger), how many, whether they use condoms, and whether they've had the HPV vaccine — and then estimates the chance they'd contract one or more STIs that year. It "rolls the dice" to show a possible outcome and lets students tweak one variable at a time to see how risk moves.

It is a teaching model built from published, population-level statistics, not a diagnostic tool. It cannot predict any real person's risk. The goal is to make the relationships between behavior and risk visible and explorable — especially the outsized roles of partner count, condoms (for fluid-borne STIs), and the HPV vaccine.

How the math works

The model is built per STI rather than as one aggregate number. For each STI and each partner, the chance that partner transmits it to the student is:

risk_per_partner = [ prevalence × tier_multiplier ] × transmission(anatomy_pairing) × condom_factor × vaccine_factor
  • prevalence — the chance a random partner is currently carrying that STI (based on young-adult prevalence; see Table 1).
  • tier_multiplier — how likely that kind of partner is to be carrying something, relative to an anonymous stranger (Table 3).
  • transmission — the per-partnership chance the infection crosses to the student, which depends on the anatomy pairing (Table 2).
  • condom_factor — the reduction from consistent condom use (Table 1).
  • vaccine_factor — the reduction from the HPV vaccine (HPV only).

Across a year with N partners, and then across all STIs, assuming independent partners:

P(get STI s) = 1 − ∏partners ( 1 − risk_per_partner )
P(any STI) = 1 − ∏STIs ( 1 − P(get STI s) )

The HPV vaccine

When a student reports having the HPV vaccine, HPV transmission is multiplied by 0.10 — a 90% reduction. This figure focuses on the high-risk, cancer-causing HPV types the vaccine targets (HPV 16, 18 and others), which are the strains that matter most for long-term health. Because risk compounds across multiple partners, the resulting drop in yearly HPV probability is large but slightly below a flat 90%.

Rolling the outcome

When a student rolls the dice, each STI is rolled independently against its own probability. A student can therefore come away with zero, one, or several STIs in a year — and the result screen shows every STI that lands, titling the box "You contracted multiple STIs" when more than one does. This is more realistic than forcing a single result and lets rarer STIs surface on their own merits rather than always being eclipsed by the most common one.

Worked example

A straight woman (vulva; partners have penises), 3 strangers, no condoms, unvaccinated → about a 58% chance of an STI that year, dominated by HPV. Turning condoms on drops it to ~36% — chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis and HIV nearly vanish, but HPV stays high because condoms barely affect it. The HPV vaccine is the bigger lever here: it drops the unprotected case to ~17%, and condoms plus the vaccine together reach ~7%. The contrast — condoms for fluid-borne STIs, the vaccine for HPV — is the central teaching point.

Assumptions & caveats

  • All figures are educational estimates calibrated to U.S. public-health data; they cannot predict any individual's real risk.
  • Prevalence figures are general young-adult prevalence. Real "hook-up pool" prevalence varies by community — and syphilis and HIV concentrate heavily in specific networks (e.g. among men who have sex with men), so the general-population figures understate risk in those contexts.
  • Per-partnership transmission values bake in an "average" amount of sex per partner, so the tool does not ask about frequency. Oral sex is not modeled separately.
  • Condom protection is modeled in two tiers (≈98% for fluid-borne STIs, ≈40% for skin-contact HPV/herpes) and the HPV-vaccine effect as a flat 90% reduction. Both are deliberate simplifications of figures that vary by study, consistency of use, and vaccine type and timing.
  • Partners are treated as independent draws; the student is assumed to start the year uninfected; and no mid-year testing or treatment is modeled.

Using this in your classroom

This tool is free to use and adapt for non-commercial educational purposes. All of the parameters above live in a single, readable file (riskModel.js) so you can review or adjust any number for your own curriculum. We encourage pairing it with a conversation about the limits of population models and the importance of testing, communication, and consent.

Source code & adoption: the entire project is open-source on GitHub — fork it, adapt it for your classroom, review every parameter, or open an issue at github.com/rossdavilla/hookup-sti-risk-game.

Disclaimer. The Hook-Up Game is an educational model using simplified, population-level estimates. It cannot predict any real individual's risk and is not medical advice.
